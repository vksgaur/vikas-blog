In the year 1400, if you had to bet on which civilisation would conquer the world, Europe would have been last on your list. China had the largest economy, the most sophisticated bureaucracy, and a navy that made European ships look like toys. India's textile industry clothed half the planet. The Islamic world held more accumulated knowledge than all of Christendom.

Europe was a rain-soaked backwater of feuding kingdoms, periodic plague, and a Church that burned people for suggesting the Earth might not be at the centre of the universe.

And yet.

By 1900, European nations controlled 84% of the Earth's land surface. The Industrial Revolution — happening in Britain and spreading through Europe — produced a gap in material wealth that had no precedent in human history.

## The Admission

The key insight is counterintuitive. Europe's greatest intellectual achievement was not any specific discovery. It was the formal acknowledgement that *we do not already know everything*.

The Scientific Method — worked out by Bacon, Descartes, Galileo, and Newton across the 17th century — is essentially the institutionalisation of doubt:

> I will not accept what I was told. I will observe. I will formulate a hypothesis. I will design an experiment that could prove my hypothesis wrong. I will publish my methods so others can attempt to prove me wrong.

This sounds obvious now. In 1650, it was radical to the point of being dangerous.

Every other intellectual tradition in human history had produced brilliant individual thinkers. Al-Haytham's optics were extraordinary. Aryabhata's mathematics were generations ahead. The Chinese had invented more than anyone else. But none of these traditions had built an *institution* that specifically rewarded systematic doubt over inherited certainty.

They had produced geniuses. They had not produced a machine for generating new knowledge.

## The Machine

The founding of the Royal Society in London in 1660 was the moment Europe built that machine. Its motto: *Nullius in verba* — take nobody's word for it.

Once you build a truth-generating machine, something deeply non-linear happens to the rate at which knowledge accumulates. **It compounds.** And compounding, as any investor knows, is the most powerful force in the universe.

The lesson of Europe's rise is not that Europe was superior. The lesson is that specific institutions — freely pursued doubt, competition between ideas, the publication of knowledge so others can replicate and improve on it — produce compounding advantages over time.

And those institutions are available to any society willing to build them.
